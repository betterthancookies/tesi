\section*{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item SAGE-smart is now \textbf{client-driven}: the server picks \emph{who}
        trains and proposes a range of work; each client decides \emph{how much}
        to train ($E$, $B$) with its own up-to-date state.
  \item The client--server protocol follows the professor's note: we combine
        \textbf{option 2} (server-side estimate) and \textbf{option 3} (the
        client accepts or refuses the proposal, refusals are replaced).
        Option 1 (broadcast) was tested and discarded: too expensive.
  \item A message is no longer free: every radio activation costs about
        \SI{12.6}{\joule} (LTE, Huang et al.\ 2012). This applies to all
        algorithms.
  \item New experimental design: every algorithm (SAGE, ESCS-SD, SAGE-smart)
        runs in three \textbf{worlds} that differ only in the \textbf{battery
        mounted on the devices}: linear, Peukert, datasheet. Tiers, workload,
        idle and recharge are the same. FedAvg and FedProx run with the linear
        battery. Results: 33 runs, one seed, three $\beta$.
  \item The datasheet battery has been re-digitised from the professor's
        document: the old tables were up to \SI{0.1}{\volt} too low.
  \item \textbf{Main result}: the battery changes the outcome much less than
        the algorithm. With any battery SAGE-smart depletes \textbf{0} devices
        and uses about half the energy; FedAvg depletes about 15, ESCS-SD 6--7,
        SAGE 0--6. Changing the battery moves at most about 2 devices; the
        Peukert battery is the most forgiving (the same work costs 12--15\,\%
        less charge), the datasheet one behaves almost like the linear one.
\end{itemize}

%======================================================================
\section{The problem: the server selects with old information}

The server chooses clients by their state of charge (SoC). But the SoC it knows
is the one the client reported the \emph{last time it took part}, possibly 10
rounds ago. In the meantime the client has consumed energy in idle, or has
been recharged, or its workload has changed. The server is therefore choosing
with stale information.

The professor's note proposes three ways to fix this:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
option & what happens & cost \\
\midrule
1 -- broadcast & the server asks \emph{every} client for its state & one message per client per round \\
2 -- estimate  & the server extrapolates the SoC from the last report & zero \\
3 -- accept / refuse & the selected client checks its own state and can refuse & one message per candidate \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

%======================================================================
\section{How much does a message cost?}

With an energy model in J/MB only, a 1\,KB status message would cost about
\SI{0.002}{\joule}: asking the state would look free. On a phone it is not.
On LTE, every isolated transmission wakes the radio up: it pays a
\emph{promotion} to the connected state, and then the radio stays on for a
\emph{tail} before going back to idle. Values from Huang et al.\ (MobiSys 2012,
Table 3):

\[
  E_{\text{session}} =
  \underbrace{\SI{1.21}{\watt}\times\SI{0.26}{\second}}_{\text{promotion}}
  + \underbrace{\SI{1.06}{\watt}\times\SI{11.58}{\second}}_{\text{tail}}
  \approx \SI{12.6}{\joule}
\]

regardless of the message size. Consequences:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Model exchange} (all algorithms): download and upload are two
        sessions, separated by the training. Cost per participant per round:
        \SI{4}{\joule} of payload $+ 2\times\SI{12.6}{\joule} \approx \SI{29}{\joule}$
        (it was \SI{4}{\joule}).
  \item \textbf{Control message} (proposal and answer): \SI{12.6}{\joule} if it is
        isolated, about \SI{0.002}{\joule} if the model download follows right
        after, because the radio is already on.
  \item \textbf{Delay}: each exchange delays the round by promotion $+$ RTT
        $\approx \SI{0.26}{\second} + \SI{0.07}{\second} = \SI{0.33}{\second}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Testing the three options}

All options were implemented and compared on a logic simulator (real battery
physics, fake training), 30 clients, 6 selected per round, 250 rounds.
\enquote{State error} is the mean gap between the SoC the server believes and
the real one.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & state error & extra energy (250 rounds) & wasted downloads \\
\midrule
stale (no protocol) & \SI{0.94}{\percent} & 0 & -- \\
1 -- broadcast & 0 & \SI{21}{\watt\hour} ($\approx\SI{12}{\percent}$ of total) & 0 \\
2 -- estimate & \SI{0.89}{\percent} & 0 & 366$^{*}$ \\
2 $+$ 3 & \SI{0.88}{\percent} & \SI{1.7}{\watt\hour}$^{*}$ & 0$^{*}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\smallskip
{\footnotesize $^{*}$ stress test with the reserve raised to 0.62, to force refusals.}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Option 1} gives perfect information, but each round the $\approx 24$
clients asked and then not selected pay a full radio session for nothing.

\paragraph{Option 2} is free but blind to recharges and to workload changes
(the note says it too: the SoC can be estimated, the load cannot). Without
recharge its error drops from \SI{0.44}{\percent} to \SI{0.28}{\percent}: almost
all of its error comes from recharges it cannot see.

\paragraph{Option 3} lets a client refuse \emph{before} downloading the model.
In the stress test, the estimate alone produced 366 clients that downloaded the
model and then had to withdraw; with $2+3$ the 484 refusals all happened before
the download, were replaced, and nobody withdrew.

%======================================================================
\section{Our choice: option 2 $+$ option 3}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Option 2 ranks everybody for free.} For most clients the
        estimate is good enough, because the battery changes slowly (one
        epoch costs about \SI{0.2}{\percent} of the battery).
  \item \textbf{Option 3 refreshes only the clients that matter}: the
        candidates. They answer with their current state and can refuse
        before the model is sent.
  \item \textbf{Cost}: a client that accepts pays $\approx\SI{0.002}{\joule}$, one
        that refuses pays \SI{12.6}{\joule}. At most 2 waves of proposals per
        round ($\approx\SI{0.33}{\second}$ each).
  \item \textbf{No starvation}: a refusal counts as a contact, so its
        staleness bonus restarts from zero and it is not asked again at the
        next round.
\end{itemize}

What it implies: real Flower messages (type \texttt{query}, with a new handler
on the client); if nobody accepts after 2 waves the run ends as \enquote{pool
exhausted}; a small gap remains between the answer and the training (the
workload may change), so a client can still withdraw at training time.

%======================================================================
\section{How SAGE-smart works now, step by step}

\paragraph{0. Subscription (once).} The server records the static data of each
client: tier, capacity, speed, dataset size, initial SoC. From these it computes
the \textbf{round deadline} (median time of 5 epochs at $B=128$, about
\SI{100}{\second} here) and the \textbf{batches to propose}: the energy-optimal
batch of every device rounds to 32, so the proposal is $B\in\{32,64\}$.

\paragraph{1. Estimate (option 2).} For every client, with no messages, the
server drains the last reported SoC with the idle power, using the battery
model of the device (linear, Peukert or datasheet, a static datum of the
subscription like the capacity):
\[
  \mathrm{SoC}_{\text{est}} = \mathrm{SoC}_{\text{last}}
  - \Delta\mathrm{SoC}_{\text{battery}}\big(P_{\text{idle}}(w_{\text{last}}),\,\Delta t\big),
  \qquad P_{\text{idle}}(w) = \SI{0.1}{\watt} + w\,(\SI{3}{\watt}-\SI{0.1}{\watt}).
\]

\paragraph{2. Ranking.}
\[
  \text{score} = a\,\mathrm{SoC}_{\text{est}} + b\,D + c\,\min\!\left(1, \tfrac{\text{rounds since last selection}}{25}\right)
\]
with $D$ the class divergence, $c=0.3$, and $(a,b)$ tuned per $\beta$. The
number of selected clients grows from 2 at round 1 to 6 from round 5 on.

\paragraph{3. Proposal (option 3).} Each candidate receives, without the model:
$E\in[E_{\text{lo}},E_{\text{hi}}]$ with $E_{\text{lo}}=2$ and
$E_{\text{hi}}=\min(10,\ E_{\text{deadline}},\ E_{\text{energy}})$, the batches
$\{32,64\}$, the deadline and the reserve (\SI{20}{\percent}).

\paragraph{4. Accept or refuse.} The client reads its current SoC, workload and
charging state, and accepts if it can do at least 2 epochs and stay above
\SI{20}{\percent}. Refusals are replaced by the next ones in the ranking.

\paragraph{5. The client decides (the core of the thesis).} After receiving the
model, the client:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item measures how useful it is: the loss $L$ of the global model on 256 local samples,
        $u=\min(1, L/\ln 10)$ ($\ln 10 \approx 2.30$ is the loss of random guessing);
  \item measures how much battery it can spend: $h=(\mathrm{SoC}-0.2)/0.8$ ($h=1$ when charging);
  \item chooses $E^{*} = E_{\text{lo}} + \operatorname{round}\big((E_{\text{hi}}-E_{\text{lo}})\,u\,h\big)$;
  \item goes down from $E^{*}$ until the predicted SoC \emph{at the end of the round}
        stays above \SI{20}{\percent} and the time, computed with the
        \emph{current} workload, fits the deadline. Among $\{32,64\}$ it takes
        the batch that leaves more charge.
\end{enumerate}
Example measured: with the same deadline, a client with no workload chooses
$E=5$; with \SI{10}{\percent} workload $E=4$; with \SI{30}{\percent}
workload $E=3$. The server could not know this: this is the \emph{late binding}.

\paragraph{6. Answer and aggregation.} The client sends the update and declares
$E$, $B$, the steps done, the initial and \emph{final SoC}, the loss before and
after. The server checks that $(E,B)$ is inside the proposal and aggregates
with FedNova, using the declared steps $\tau_i$:
\[
  x \leftarrow x + \tau_{\text{eff}} \sum_i p_i \frac{\Delta_i}{\tau_i},
  \qquad \tau_{\text{eff}}=\sum_i p_i\tau_i .
\]

\paragraph{7. Update.} The declared final SoC becomes the new starting point
for the estimate of step 1.

\section{Three batteries for the devices}

\textbf{New this week.} Last week there was one real battery (datasheet) and
the algorithms \emph{estimated} the SoC with different models. Now each run
takes place in a \textbf{world} where all devices mount the same type of
battery, and every algorithm reads the SoC of that battery, as a phone does.
Tiers, workload, idle and recharge are identical in the three worlds, and the
same seed gives the same population. The only difference is how the battery
turns power into lost charge:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{lin} (linear): $\Delta\mathrm{SoC} = P\,t / (V_{\text{nom}} C)$,
        with $V_{\text{nom}} = \SI{3.7}{\volt}$;
  \item \textbf{peuk} (Peukert, $n = 1.15$): the battery lasts $C'/I^{n}$ hours
        at current $I$, so low currents are cheap;
  \item \textbf{nm} (datasheet): $P = V(\mathrm{SoC}, I)\,I$ is solved for the
        current, and the usable capacity $Q(I)$ shrinks at high current.
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule

\midrule
FedAvg, FedProx & \texttt{fedavg}, \texttt{fedprox} & -- & -- \\
SAGE            & \texttt{sage\_lin}$^{*}$  & \texttt{sage\_peuk}        & \texttt{sage\_nm} \\
SAGE-smart      & \texttt{sage\_smart\_lin} & \texttt{sage\_smart\_peuk} & \texttt{sage\_smart\_nm} \\
ESCS-SD         & \texttt{escs\_sd\_lin}    & \texttt{escs\_sd\_peuk}    & \texttt{escs\_sd\_nm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\smallskip
{\footnotesize $^{*}$ \texttt{sage\_lin} is the SAGE of the paper (weights
$a,b,c$, renewable term, eligibility 0.2); \texttt{sage\_peuk} and
\texttt{sage\_nm} are our ablation (weights $a,b$ only). For SAGE, linear vs
the other two changes the algorithm too.}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{SAGE-smart}: the client predicts its consumption with its own
        battery, and the server uses the same battery model in the estimate of
        step 1. Envelope, negotiation and FedNova are identical in the three
        worlds.
  \item \textbf{Datasheet tables re-digitised.} The curves of the professor's
        document were digitised pixel by pixel (error about \SI{0.01}{\volt}).
        The old tables were \SIrange{0.07}{0.10}{\volt} too low between SoC 0.65
        and 0.9 and had the final knee too early. Capacities $Q(I)$ are those of
        the document (2.60 / 2.50 / \SI{2.39}{\ampere\hour}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How long does a full battery last?}
\label{sec:physics}

Battery alone, constant power, \SI{3500}{\milli\ampere\hour} cell, hours from
100\,\% to 0\,\% relative to the linear battery:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
power & \SI{0.1}{\watt} (idle) & \SI{1}{\watt} (idle, busy phone) & \SI{2}{\watt} (training, $B=32$) & \SI{3}{\watt} (training, $B=128$) \\
\midrule
linear    & 1.00 & 1.00 & 1.00 & 1.00 \\
Peukert   & \textbf{1.63} & 1.15 & 1.04 & 0.98 \\
datasheet & 1.01 & 1.01 & 1.01 & 1.00 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Peukert} is very generous at low current: a phone at rest lasts
        63\,\% longer than with the linear battery. Since idle is about half of
        all the energy, a Peukert world is a kinder world.
  \item \textbf{Datasheet} lasts as long as the linear battery, because
        \SI{3.7}{\volt} is almost exactly its average voltage (\SI{3.72}{\volt}).
        But it is not linear inside: compared with the linear battery it is
        7\,\% cheaper between 100\,\% and 60\,\% (high voltage) and 9--11\,\%
        more expensive below 20\,\% (low voltage).
\end{itemize}

\section{What changes when the battery changes}

\subsection{The same work costs a different amount of charge}

Ratio between the charge paid by the battery and the energy used by the device
(charge / load in the table):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & SAGE & SAGE-smart & ESCS-SD \\
\midrule
linear    & 1.00 & 1.00 & 1.00 \\
Peukert   & 0.87 & 0.85 & 0.88 \\
datasheet & 0.99 & 0.97 & 1.00 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

With Peukert the same work costs 12--15\,\% less charge, almost all of it in
idle (SAGE-smart: \SI{72}{\watt\hour} of idle charge against 90--92). The
datasheet battery is within 3\,\% of the linear one, as predicted in
Section~\ref{sec:physics}: SAGE-smart keeps its devices above half charge,
where the datasheet battery is slightly cheaper (0.97); ESCS-SD runs them
down, where it is more expensive, and the two effects cancel (1.00).

\subsection{Depleted devices: small changes}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{SAGE-smart}: 0 in all three worlds.
  \item \textbf{SAGE} (ablation): 0 with Peukert, 1.7 with the datasheet battery.
        SAGE of the paper with the linear battery: 5.7 (but it is another
        algorithm).
  \item \textbf{ESCS-SD}: 6.0 / 5.7 / 7.0, and the order changes from one
        $\beta$ to another. Peukert does not help ESCS-SD: its filter
        (\enquote{SoC after the round $\ge 20\,\%$}) lets more devices in when
        batteries look fuller, so it \emph{spends} the saved charge on more
        training (load \SI{334}{\watt\hour} against 316).
\end{itemize}
The battery moves at most about 2 devices; the algorithm moves up to 15.

\subsection{Final state of charge: Peukert keeps batteries fuller}

SAGE-smart 0.59 with Peukert against 0.56 / 0.55; SAGE 0.45 with Peukert
against 0.36. ESCS-SD stays at 0.33 in all worlds, for the reason above.

\subsection{Accuracy: mostly unchanged, with one real effect}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ESCS-SD}: flat (0.547 / 0.556 / 0.559).
  \item \textbf{SAGE-smart}: equal at $\beta=0.5$ and $1.0$ (within 0.014).
        The gap at $\beta=0.1$ (0.402 with the datasheet battery against 0.50)
        is noise: last week, with almost the same physics, the same arm scored
        0.535. At $\beta=0.1$ one seed is not enough.
  \item \textbf{SAGE}: Peukert 0.539 against datasheet 0.483, at every $\beta$.
        This one is real. The two runs are equal for 200 rounds; then, with the
        datasheet battery, the devices are emptier (mean SoC 0.34--0.41 against
        0.43--0.48 in the last 50 rounds), more of them fall below 0.3 and get
        half epochs, the training done per round drops (\SI{0.35}{\watt\hour}
        against \SI{0.53}{\watt\hour} at $\beta=0.5$) and accuracy goes down in
        the last 50 rounds (0.556 against 0.603 at $\beta=0.5$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Is this expected?}

Yes.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Peukert with $n=1.15$ makes low currents cheap, and idle is a low
        current: a Peukert world is the most forgiving one, and the algorithms
        that do not spend the saved charge (SAGE, SAGE-smart) end with fuller
        batteries.
  \item The datasheet battery behaves like the linear one on average, so it
        changes little; it is harsher only when batteries are low, which is
        where SAGE ends its runs.
  \item The re-digitised tables changed almost nothing: compared with last
        week's datasheet arms, ESCS-SD goes from 7.3 to 7.0 depleted devices,
        SAGE from 2.7 to 1.7, SAGE-smart stays at 0.
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}

\toprule

\subsection{SAGE-smart in detail}

\begin{itemize}
  \item No depleted device, final SoC 0.55--0.59, and about \textbf{half the
        charge} of the others (130--\SI{150}{\watt\hour} against
        238--\SI{316}{\watt\hour}), with the best accuracy.
  \item Clients choose about 2.2 epochs at $\beta=0.5$ and $1.0$ and 3.3--3.6
        at $\beta=0.1$, with $B \approx 37$--43 (a mix of 32 and 64).
  \item Negotiation: 0--5 refusals out of about 1490 proposals per run, no
        withdrawals.
  \item The SoC declared by the client is exactly the real one in all three
        worlds; the server estimate of step 1 is wrong by 0.4--2.3 points of
        SoC on average (recharges and workload changes it cannot see).
  \item 9--21\,\% of participations exceed the deadline (about
        \SI{100}{\second}): slow devices that cannot do even 2 epochs in time.
  \item Caveat: the comparison is at the same number of rounds. SAGE-smart
        rounds are shorter, so its devices spend less time in idle (72--92
        against 128--\SI{185}{\watt\hour} of idle charge).
\end{itemize}

\section{Limits and next steps}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One seed}: at $\beta=0.1$ accuracy moves by up to 0.1 between
        runs with almost the same physics. Run seeds 7 and 13.
  \item \textbf{FedAvg and FedProx only with the linear battery}: comparing
        them with the Peukert and datasheet arms mixes algorithm and battery.
        One more label (\texttt{fedavg\_nm}) would give a common reference.
  \item \textbf{SAGE linear is the paper algorithm}: linear vs the other two
        SAGE arms changes both algorithm and battery.
  \item \textbf{Peukert exponent}: $n=1.15$ is a lead-acid value; for lithium
        cells the literature gives 1.01--1.10, so the Peukert world is
        optimistic. A run with $n=1.05$ would show how much.
  \item \textbf{Deadline}: 9--21\,\% of SAGE-smart participations miss it.
  \item \textbf{Same rounds or same time?} SAGE-smart rounds are shorter.
        Compare also at the same wall-clock time or the same energy budget.
  \item \textbf{Where does the SAGE-smart gain come from?} Ablations: without
        late binding, without deadline, without the loss term, with $B=128$.
  \item \textbf{Fair amount of work}: baselines always do $E=5$, $B=128$;
        SAGE-smart about 2--3 epochs. Add a baseline with the same number of
        epochs and compare energy per accuracy.
\end{itemize}
