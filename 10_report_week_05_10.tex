%======================================================================
\section*{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item SAGE-smart is now \textbf{client-driven}: the server picks \emph{who}
        trains and proposes a range of work; each client decides \emph{how much}
        to train ($E$, $B$) with its own up-to-date state.
  \item The client--server protocol follows the professor's note: we combine
        \textbf{option 2} (server-side estimate) and \textbf{option 3} (the
        client accepts or refuses the proposal, refusals are replaced).
        Option 1 (broadcast) was tested and discarded: too expensive.
  \item A message is no longer free: every radio activation costs about
        \SI{12.6}{\joule} (LTE, Huang et al.\ 2012). This applies to all
        algorithms.
  \item Every algorithm (SAGE, ESCS-SD, SAGE-smart) now runs with four ways of
        knowing the battery: the accounting of the papers, a linear fuel gauge,
        a Peukert fuel gauge and the true SoC (datasheet). Results: 39 runs,
        one seed, three $\beta$.
  \item \textbf{Main result}: what reduces depleted devices is \emph{counting
        idle and recharge} (ESCS-SD: 11.7 $\rightarrow$ 9.0; SAGE: 11.7
        $\rightarrow$ 2.0), not the shape of the battery curve (linear
        $\rightarrow$ datasheet: from 0 to $-1.7$ devices). The linear gauge is
        wrong by less than one point of SoC, because \SI{3.7}{\volt} is the
        average voltage of the cell.
\end{itemize}

%======================================================================
\section{The problem: the server selects with old information}

The server chooses clients by their state of charge (SoC). But the SoC it knows
is the one the client reported the \emph{last time it took part}, possibly 10
rounds ago. In the meantime the client has consumed energy in idle, or has
been recharged, or its workload has changed. The server is therefore choosing
with stale information.

The professor's note proposes three ways to fix this:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
option & what happens & cost \\
\midrule
1 -- broadcast & the server asks \emph{every} client for its state & one message per client per round \\
2 -- estimate  & the server extrapolates the SoC from the last report & zero \\
3 -- accept / refuse & the selected client checks its own state and can refuse & one message per candidate \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

%======================================================================
\section{How much does a message cost?}

With an energy model in J/MB only, a 1\,KB status message would cost about
\SI{0.002}{\joule}: asking the state would look free. On a phone it is not.
On LTE, every isolated transmission wakes the radio up: it pays a
\emph{promotion} to the connected state, and then the radio stays on for a
\emph{tail} before going back to idle. Values from Huang et al.\ (MobiSys 2012,
Table 3):

\[
  E_{\text{session}} =
  \underbrace{\SI{1.21}{\watt}\times\SI{0.26}{\second}}_{\text{promotion}}
  + \underbrace{\SI{1.06}{\watt}\times\SI{11.58}{\second}}_{\text{tail}}
  \approx \SI{12.6}{\joule}
\]

regardless of the message size. Consequences:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Model exchange} (all algorithms): download and upload are two
        sessions, separated by the training. Cost per participant per round:
        \SI{4}{\joule} of payload $+ 2\times\SI{12.6}{\joule} \approx \SI{29}{\joule}$
        (it was \SI{4}{\joule}).
  \item \textbf{Control message} (proposal and answer): \SI{12.6}{\joule} if it is
        isolated, about \SI{0.002}{\joule} if the model download follows right
        after, because the radio is already on.
  \item \textbf{Delay}: each exchange delays the round by promotion $+$ RTT
        $\approx \SI{0.26}{\second} + \SI{0.07}{\second} = \SI{0.33}{\second}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Testing the three options}

All options were implemented and compared on a logic simulator (real battery
physics, fake training), 30 clients, 6 selected per round, 250 rounds.
\enquote{State error} is the mean gap between the SoC the server believes and
the real one.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & state error & extra energy (250 rounds) & wasted downloads \\
\midrule
stale (no protocol) & \SI{0.94}{\percent} & 0 & -- \\
1 -- broadcast & 0 & \SI{21}{\watt\hour} ($\approx\SI{12}{\percent}$ of total) & 0 \\
2 -- estimate & \SI{0.89}{\percent} & 0 & 366$^{*}$ \\
2 $+$ 3 & \SI{0.88}{\percent} & \SI{1.7}{\watt\hour}$^{*}$ & 0$^{*}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\smallskip
{\footnotesize $^{*}$ stress test with the reserve raised to 0.62, to force refusals.}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Option 1} gives perfect information, but each round the $\approx 24$
clients asked and then not selected pay a full radio session for nothing.

\paragraph{Option 2} is free but blind to recharges and to workload changes
(the note says it too: the SoC can be estimated, the load cannot). Without
recharge its error drops from \SI{0.44}{\percent} to \SI{0.28}{\percent}: almost
all of its error comes from recharges it cannot see.

\paragraph{Option 3} lets a client refuse \emph{before} downloading the model.
In the stress test, the estimate alone produced 366 clients that downloaded the
model and then had to withdraw; with $2+3$ the 484 refusals all happened before
the download, were replaced, and nobody withdrew.

%======================================================================
\section{Our choice: option 2 $+$ option 3}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Option 2 ranks everybody for free.} For most clients the
        estimate is good enough, because the battery changes slowly (one
        epoch costs about \SI{0.2}{\percent} of the battery).
  \item \textbf{Option 3 refreshes only the clients that matter}: the
        candidates. They answer with their current state and can refuse
        before the model is sent.
  \item \textbf{Cost}: a client that accepts pays $\approx\SI{0.002}{\joule}$, one
        that refuses pays \SI{12.6}{\joule}. At most 2 waves of proposals per
        round ($\approx\SI{0.33}{\second}$ each).
  \item \textbf{No starvation}: a refusal counts as a contact, so its
        staleness bonus restarts from zero and it is not asked again at the
        next round.
\end{itemize}

What it implies: real Flower messages (type \texttt{query}, with a new handler
on the client); if nobody accepts after 2 waves the run ends as \enquote{pool
exhausted}; a small gap remains between the answer and the training (the
workload may change), so a client can still withdraw at training time.

%======================================================================
\section{How SAGE-smart works now, step by step}

\paragraph{0. Subscription (once).} The server records the static data of each
client: tier, capacity, speed, dataset size, initial SoC. From these it computes
the \textbf{round deadline} (median time of 5 epochs at $B=128$, about
\SI{100}{\second} here) and the \textbf{batches to propose}: the energy-optimal
batch of every device rounds to 32, so the proposal is $B\in\{32,64\}$.

\paragraph{1. Estimate (option 2).} For every client, with no messages:
\[
  \mathrm{SoC}_{\text{est}} = \mathrm{SoC}_{\text{last}}
  - \frac{P_{\text{idle}}(w_{\text{last}})\,\Delta t}{V_{\text{nom}}\,C},
  \qquad P_{\text{idle}}(w) = \SI{0.1}{\watt} + w\,(\SI{3}{\watt}-\SI{0.1}{\watt}).
\]

\paragraph{2. Ranking.}
\[
  \text{score} = a\,\mathrm{SoC}_{\text{est}} + b\,D + c\,\min\!\left(1, \tfrac{\text{rounds since last selection}}{25}\right)
\]
with $D$ the class divergence, $c=0.3$, and $(a,b)$ tuned per $\beta$. The
number of selected clients grows from 2 at round 1 to 6 from round 5 on.

\paragraph{3. Proposal (option 3).} Each candidate receives, without the model:
$E\in[E_{\text{lo}},E_{\text{hi}}]$ with $E_{\text{lo}}=2$ and
$E_{\text{hi}}=\min(10,\ E_{\text{deadline}},\ E_{\text{energy}})$, the batches
$\{32,64\}$, the deadline and the reserve (\SI{20}{\percent}).

\paragraph{4. Accept or refuse.} The client reads its current SoC, workload and
charging state, and accepts if it can do at least 2 epochs and stay above
\SI{20}{\percent}. Refusals are replaced by the next ones in the ranking.

\paragraph{5. The client decides (the core of the thesis).} After receiving the
model, the client:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item measures how useful it is: the loss $L$ of the global model on 256 local samples,
        $u=\min(1, L/\ln 10)$ ($\ln 10 \approx 2.30$ is the loss of random guessing);
  \item measures how much battery it can spend: $h=(\mathrm{SoC}-0.2)/0.8$ ($h=1$ when charging);
  \item chooses $E^{*} = E_{\text{lo}} + \operatorname{round}\big((E_{\text{hi}}-E_{\text{lo}})\,u\,h\big)$;
  \item goes down from $E^{*}$ until the predicted SoC \emph{at the end of the round}
        stays above \SI{20}{\percent} and the time, computed with the
        \emph{current} workload, fits the deadline. Among $\{32,64\}$ it takes
        the batch that leaves more charge.
\end{enumerate}
Example measured: with the same deadline, a client with no workload chooses
$E=5$; with \SI{10}{\percent} workload $E=4$; with \SI{30}{\percent}
workload $E=3$. The server could not know this: this is the \emph{late binding}.

\paragraph{6. Answer and aggregation.} The client sends the update and declares
$E$, $B$, the steps done, the initial and \emph{final SoC}, the loss before and
after. The server checks that $(E,B)$ is inside the proposal and aggregates
with FedNova, using the declared steps $\tau_i$:
\[
  x \leftarrow x + \tau_{\text{eff}} \sum_i p_i \frac{\Delta_i}{\tau_i},
  \qquad \tau_{\text{eff}}=\sum_i p_i\tau_i .
\]

\paragraph{7. Update.} The declared final SoC becomes the new starting point
for the estimate of step 1.

\section{Four ways to know the battery}

The real battery of the simulator is always the datasheet model: its voltage
and its usable capacity depend on the state of charge and on the current. What
changes is how the \emph{algorithm} estimates the SoC of each client:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{paper}: the accounting of the SAGE and ESCS papers,
        $\mathrm{SoC}_0 - (\text{Wh of the rounds the client trained})/C$.
        It does \emph{not} count idle and it does not see recharges.
  \item \textbf{lin}: a linear fuel gauge, $P\,t/(V_{\text{nom}}C)$ with
        $V_{\text{nom}}=\SI{3.7}{\volt}$, applied to \emph{every} drain
        (training, radio, idle) and reset to 100\,\% after a full recharge.
  \item \textbf{peuk}: the same fuel gauge, with Peukert's law ($n=1.15$).
  \item \textbf{nm}: the algorithm sees the true SoC of the datasheet battery.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How wrong is each gauge? (battery alone, no FL)}
\label{sec:gauge}

We drain the real battery from one SoC level to another and ask how much each
gauge thinks was consumed (\SI{3500}{\milli\ampere\hour} cell; the
\SI{4500}{\milli\ampere\hour} cell gives the same numbers):

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
 & real drain & linear & Peukert, idle (\SI{0.1}{\watt}) & Peukert, training (\SI{3}{\watt}) \\
\midrule
SoC $1.0 \rightarrow 0.6$ & 0.40 & 0.423 \;($+6$\,\%)   & 0.26 \;($-35$\,\%) & 0.43 \;($+8$\,\%) \\
SoC $0.6 \rightarrow 0.2$ & 0.40 & 0.390 \;($-2.5$\,\%) & 0.24 \;($-40$\,\%) & 0.40 \;($-1$\,\%) \\
SoC $0.2 \rightarrow 0.0$ & 0.20 & 0.178 \;($-11$\,\%)  & 0.11 \;($-45$\,\%) & 0.18 \;($-9$\,\%) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Why the linear gauge is almost right.} A real cell has a high
voltage when it is full (\SI{4.3}{\volt}) and a low one when it is nearly
empty (\SIrange{3.3}{3.5}{\volt}). The phone needs a power $P=V\,I$, so for the
same \SI{3}{\watt} it draws less current when the cell is full and more when it
is nearly empty. The linear gauge assumes \SI{3.7}{\volt} all the time, and
\SI{3.7}{\volt} is simply the \emph{average} voltage of the discharge curve
(\SI{3.67}{\volt} in our datasheet). So the linear gauge is a bit pessimistic
when the battery is full and a bit optimistic when it is nearly empty, and the
two errors cancel: starting from 100\,\%, when the real battery reaches
20\,\% the linear gauge reads 18.7\,\%, and when the real battery is empty it
reads 0.9\,\%. The largest gap over a whole discharge is 2.3 points of SoC.

The other non-linear effect, losing capacity at high current, does not show
up: in our simulation a phone draws at most about 0.2C during training
(\SI{3}{\watt} on a \SI{3500}{\milli\ampere\hour} cell) and about 0.01C in idle,
while the datasheet loses capacity only above 0.2C (about $-4$\,\% at 0.5C).

\paragraph{Why Peukert is optimistic.} With $n=1.15$ Peukert is fine during
training, but in idle it sees only 55--65\,\% of the real consumption: it
believes that slow drains are almost free. Since idle is about half of all the
energy (Section~\ref{sec:results}), a Peukert gauge overestimates the SoC.

\section{What changes when the battery model changes}

\subsection{Step A: counting idle and recharge --- a large effect}

\paragraph{ESCS-SD (clean comparison).} From the paper accounting to the
linear gauge, depleted devices go from 11.7 to 9.0, and they go down in every
$\beta$ (12 $\rightarrow$ 11, 11 $\rightarrow$ 7, 12 $\rightarrow$ 9). The
first death comes 18--24 rounds later.

\paragraph{SAGE.} From 11.7 to 2.0 depleted devices; the first death moves
from round 118--138 to round 219--241, or never. Part of this jump comes from
the different weights and rules of the paper version, but the direction is the
same as in ESCS.

\paragraph{Why.} Idle is about half of all the energy (SAGE paper: 152 of
\SI{277}{\watt\hour}; ESCS paper: 140 of \SI{284}{\watt\hour}), and the paper
accounting does not see it: by the end of the run, 30--38\,\% of SoC per
device is missing from its books. The selector believes the devices are fuller
than they are, keeps choosing the same ones and depletes them.

\paragraph{Two traps when reading the table.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Final SoC.} ESCS paper ends with a \emph{higher} mean SoC than
        ESCS lin (0.33 vs 0.28) although it depletes more devices. The paper
        accounting concentrates the drain on a few devices, which die, and
        leaves the others fuller: the survivors end at 0.54 on average, against
        0.40 with lin and 0.42 with nm (SAGE: 0.54 against 0.41--0.42). The mean
        SoC must always be read together with the number of depleted devices.
  \item \textbf{Energy.} The paper arms seem to use less energy (ESCS: 284 vs
        \SI{319}{\watt\hour}) only because dead devices stop consuming in idle
        (140 vs \SI{173}{\watt\hour} of idle).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Step B: the shape of the battery curve --- a small effect}

\paragraph{SAGE.} 2.0 / 2.3 / 2.7 depleted devices with lin / peuk / nm, and
accuracy 0.510 / 0.515 / 0.513: no trend. Per $\beta$ the order even flips
(at $\beta=0.1$ lin is the worst with 5 depleted devices, at $\beta=1.0$ it is
the best with 0). This is the noise level of a single seed.

\paragraph{SAGE-smart.} No depleted device with any of the three, final SoC
0.56--0.57, energy 147--\SI{149}{\watt\hour}. Accuracy 0.620 / 0.631 / 0.633:
the only gap is at $\beta=0.1$ (0.498 / 0.520 / 0.535), where the loss is
higher, clients train more and batteries get closer to the reserve. At
$\beta=0.5$ and $1.0$ the three are within 0.007.

\paragraph{ESCS-SD.} 9.0 / 9.3 / 7.3: the only algorithm where the true SoC
clearly helps. With nm there are 1--2 fewer depleted devices than with lin in
every $\beta$ (11 $\rightarrow$ 9, 7 $\rightarrow$ 6, 9 $\rightarrow$ 7).
Peukert is not better than linear.

\paragraph{What does not change.} Accuracy has no consistent link with the
battery model (gaps of 0.01--0.03 that flip between $\beta$, the largest at
$\beta=0.1$, the noisiest regime). Energy is the same within each algorithm
(SAGE 270--276, SAGE-smart 147--149, ESCS 315--\SI{320}{\watt\hour}): the
battery model changes \emph{who} is selected, not how much work is done.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The linear gauge is wrong by less than one point of SoC.} It
        is slightly pessimistic while batteries are above half, and becomes
        optimistic only when they are low, as predicted in
        Section~\ref{sec:gauge}.
  \item \textbf{SAGE and SAGE-smart use the SoC mainly to rank clients, and
        keep a margin} (20\,\% reserve; SAGE-smart even predicts the SoC at the
        end of the round). An error of half a point moves all clients in the
        same way, so the ranking, and therefore the selection, does not change.
  \item \textbf{ESCS works close to empty.} Its only battery check is
        \enquote{SoC $\geq 20\,\%$} before the round, and in the second half of
        the run many of its devices sit around that threshold (median final
        SoC 0.13--0.15). That is exactly where the linear gauge becomes
        optimistic, so it lets in devices that are actually a bit lower: 1--2
        extra depleted devices compared with the true SoC.
  \item \textbf{Peukert overestimates the SoC by up to 3 points}, because it
        sees idle as almost free. But it does so for all clients alike, so the
        ranking barely changes, and in SAGE-smart the 20\,\% reserve absorbs it.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Is this expected?}

Yes, for three reasons:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $V_{\text{nom}}$ is by definition the average voltage of the cell, so a
        linear gauge is right on average and fails only at the two ends of the
        curve.
  \item Federated training is a light load for a phone battery (at most about
        0.2C): the rate effects that make a battery non-linear appear at higher
        currents.
  \item The only place where we see a difference (ESCS, near empty) is exactly
        where the linear model is expected to fail.
\end{itemize}
The shape of the curve would matter more with higher currents (heavier models,
smaller or aged batteries), in the cold, or with policies that deliberately run
devices close to empty.

\smallskip
{\footnotesize $^{*}$ also different weights and rules.}
\end{center}

Order of importance: \textbf{the policy} $>$ \textbf{counting every drain}
$>$ \textbf{the shape of the battery curve}. For the thesis this means that, in
this setting, a simple linear gauge on the client is enough, \emph{provided it
counts every drain}; what really matters is what the client does with it.

\subsection{SAGE-smart compared with the others}

No depleted device with any battery model, final SoC $\approx 0.57$, and about
\textbf{half the energy} of the others (\SI{148}{\watt\hour} vs
270--\SI{320}{\watt\hour}). The negotiation stayed almost idle: 0--5 refusals
per run, no withdrawals, control energy below \SI{0.02}{\watt\hour}.
One caveat: the comparison is at the same number of rounds. SAGE-smart rounds
are shorter (deadline, about 2--3 epochs), so over 250 rounds its devices spend
less time in idle (90 vs 165--\SI{173}{\watt\hour} of idle energy). Part of its
survival comes from this.

\section{Limits and next steps}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One seed}: step A is clear (it holds in every $\beta$); the
        differences of step B (1--2 devices) are at the noise level. Run seeds
        7 and 13.
  \item \textbf{Light load}: the result holds for currents up to about 0.2C. A
        stress test with smaller batteries or a heavier model should show
        where the shape of the curve starts to matter.
  \item \textbf{Gauge error per device}: today only the mean over all clients
        is logged, and depleted clients pollute it. Log the believed and the
        true SoC of every client.
  \item \textbf{Clean step A for SAGE}: an arm with the paper accounting and
        the same weights as \texttt{sage\_lin}, so that only the accounting
        changes, as in ESCS.
  \item \textbf{Where does the SAGE-smart gain come from?} It changes several
        things at once (deadline, fewer epochs, $B=32$, FedNova, staleness,
        client decision). Ablations: without late binding, without deadline,
        without the loss term.
  \item \textbf{Same rounds or same time?} SAGE-smart rounds are shorter.
        Compare also at the same wall-clock time or the same energy budget.
  \item \textbf{Fair amount of work}: baselines always do $E=5$, $B=128$;
        SAGE-smart about 2--3 epochs. Add a baseline with the same number of
        epochs and compare energy per accuracy.
  \item \textbf{A more dynamic world}: the workload changes once per round and
        the battery drains slowly, so the information only the client has is
        worth little and the negotiation is rarely triggered.
\end{itemize}
